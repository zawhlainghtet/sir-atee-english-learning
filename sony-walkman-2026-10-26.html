Sony and the Walkman: Making Music Move

Estimated lesson time: about 20 minutes
Level: B2 Upper-Intermediate
Focus: Sony's founders, the company's growth, and the development of the Walkman

Today, carrying music is completely normal. A phone can hold thousands of songs, and wireless headphones let us listen almost anywhere. In the late 1970s, however, personal listening was very different. People could enjoy records or cassette tapes at home, and portable radios existed, but high-quality stereo music was usually connected to a room, a car, or a large machine. Sony changed that situation with a small cassette player called the Walkman.

The Walkman was important not because it introduced a completely new scientific discovery. Most of its basic technology already existed. Its achievement was to combine familiar technologies in a new way and to offer people a new habit: listening privately while walking, travelling, or relaxing outside the home. The story also shows how Sony's two founders, Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita, had different but complementary strengths.

Two Founders with Different Strengths

Sony began in Tokyo shortly after the Second World War. Japan's cities and industries had been badly damaged, and materials were difficult to obtain. In 1946, Masaru Ibuka and Akio Morita established a small company called Tokyo Telecommunications Engineering Corporation. It began with limited money and a team of around twenty people.

Ibuka was an engineer and inventor who was deeply interested in what technology could do. He enjoyed difficult technical problems and wanted to create products that had not existed before. Morita came from a family that produced sake, but he studied physics and was also fascinated by electronics. He became the stronger business communicator. He understood marketing, international customers, and the importance of giving technical ideas a clear commercial purpose.

The founders did not want their company simply to copy successful foreign products. They wanted to produce useful electronics that could create new markets. This was an ambitious goal for a young firm in a country recovering from war. Their early attempts included an electric rice cooker that did not work reliably. Failure was part of the company's history from the beginning, but so was experimentation.

From Tape Recorders to a Global Name

One of the company's first major products was a tape recorder. At that time, magnetic recording was unfamiliar to many Japanese customers. Building the machine was only one part of the challenge. The company also had to explain why anyone would need it. Schools and courts became early users because recordings allowed speech to be saved and reviewed.

During the 1950s, the company began working with transistor technology. Transistors could replace larger vacuum tubes, making electronic products smaller and more suitable for battery power. Sony developed portable transistor radios and sold them internationally. These radios helped establish an idea that later became central to the Walkman: a product could succeed by freeing entertainment from a fixed location.

In 1958, the company officially changed its name to Sony. The founders wanted a short name that was easy to pronounce in different languages. This decision showed that they were thinking globally. Sony continued to develop televisions, audio equipment, and other consumer electronics. Over time, it gained a reputation for compact design and for turning advanced engineering into products that ordinary customers could use.

The company's growth was not based on one perfect invention. It came from a series of technical and commercial decisions. Sony made some products that became famous and others that disappeared. Yet its engineers kept learning how to make components smaller, improve sound, reduce power use, and manufacture complex electronics reliably.

Ibuka's Personal Request

The Walkman story began with a practical request. Masaru Ibuka often travelled by air and wanted to listen to stereo music during long flights. Portable cassette recorders existed, but they were too heavy for comfortable personal use. Sony had a compact monaural recorder called the Pressman. Ibuka asked engineers to modify one so that it could play stereo music through headphones.

The modified machine was not yet a commercial product. It was a solution for one person. However, when Akio Morita tried it, he saw a much larger opportunity. He noticed that young people wanted to enjoy music throughout the day. A lightweight player could allow them to take their favourite songs onto a train, into a park, or through city streets.

This was an example of observing a behaviour before there was a clear market for it. Customers were not entering shops and demanding a “personal stereo.” The category did not yet exist. Morita had to imagine how people might behave if the right product became available.

A Product That Could Not Record

Inside Sony, the idea faced serious doubts. At that time, cassette machines were usually recorders as well as players. Removing the recording function appeared to make the product less useful. Some employees believed that customers would refuse to buy a machine that did less than existing recorders.

Morita saw the matter differently. The purpose of the proposed device was not to record meetings or voices. Its purpose was to make private stereo listening mobile. Removing the recording mechanism could reduce weight, size, and complexity. In product design, fewer functions can sometimes create a clearer and more enjoyable experience.

Sony also had another technology developing at the right moment: very light headphones. Older headphones were often large and heavy. A portable player would not feel truly portable if users had to wear uncomfortable equipment on their heads. Combining the compact cassette player with lightweight stereo headphones turned two separate developments into one complete experience.

Morita set an extremely short schedule. The product had to reach stores before the summer holiday season in 1979, leaving only about four months for development and preparation. To reduce technical risk, the team reused the basic mechanism of the existing Pressman instead of creating every component from the beginning. This allowed engineers to concentrate on reliability, stereo sound, controls, and the overall user experience.

The First Walkman

The first model, the TPS-L2, went on sale in Japan in July 1979. It was a blue-and-silver cassette player small enough to carry. It had no speaker and no recording function. Users listened through the included headphones. Interestingly, it had two headphone sockets, so two people could listen together, and a button lowered the music volume so they could speak without removing the headphones.

The name “Walkman” grew from the name Pressman and the idea of moving around. Some people at Sony worried that the word sounded unnatural in English. Different names were initially considered or used in overseas markets. Yet Japanese customers and foreign visitors quickly remembered the Walkman name. Morita eventually decided to use it internationally.

The launch required more than normal advertising because customers had to understand a new activity. Sony arranged demonstrations in which journalists could walk around and listen. Staff took the product to places where young people spent time. Instead of describing only its technical specifications, the company showed a lifestyle: music could become a private companion throughout the day.

Early sales were slower than expected, which seemed to support the doubters. Then public interest increased rapidly. People began seeing the device in use, and word of mouth became powerful. The first production run sold out, and the Walkman developed into an international success.

Creating a New Market

The Walkman's deepest innovation was the freedom it gave the listener. Before it, music in a public place was often shared through a radio or loudspeaker. With a Walkman, one person could create a private sound environment while remaining physically present in a crowd. Commuting, exercising, and waiting could all feel different.

This change was not welcomed by everyone. Critics worried that headphones would isolate people from one another. They also raised safety concerns when listeners could not hear traffic or warnings. Similar arguments continue today around smartphones and wireless earbuds. New products often solve one problem while creating new social questions.

Competitors soon introduced their own personal cassette players. This competition confirmed that Sony had not merely created a successful model; it had helped create an entire product category. According to Sony's official history, more than 400 million Walkman units were sold between 1979 and 2019 when cassette, CD, MiniDisc, and digital models are counted together.

The Walkman name also became part of everyday language. In 1986, it entered the Oxford English Dictionary. For a brand name to become a familiar word, the product must strongly represent an activity in people's minds. “Walkman” came to mean more than one Sony device; it represented portable personal music.

Development Beyond the Cassette

Sony continued changing the Walkman instead of treating the first model as finished. Later cassette versions became smaller, lighter, and more energy-efficient. Some were designed for sport or rough outdoor use. Auto-reverse systems allowed users to play both sides of a cassette without removing it.

As music formats changed, the brand moved to compact discs, MiniDiscs, and digital audio files. Each transition required different engineering. A portable CD player had to protect playback from vibration. MiniDisc products used a small rewritable optical disc. Digital models needed software, storage, and battery management rather than a mechanical tape system.

Not every transition was equally successful. Sony had helped define portable music, but later faced powerful competition from MP3 players and smartphones. A company can create a market and still struggle when the market changes. The Walkman story therefore contains both achievement and warning: past innovation does not guarantee future leadership.

What Founders and Engineers Can Learn

First, innovation can begin with a specific personal problem. Ibuka wanted better music during flights. That narrow request revealed a need shared by millions of people. Designers should pay attention to small frustrations because they may point toward larger opportunities.

Second, a product needs a clear purpose. The Walkman became more focused when Sony removed recording. This choice looked like a weakness when products were compared by the number of functions. In actual use, however, it helped make the device lighter and easier to understand.

Third, successful products often depend on a complete system. The player alone was not enough. Lightweight headphones, reliable cassette mechanisms, batteries, manufacturing, marketing, music availability, and a memorable name all contributed to the experience. Engineers frequently concentrate on the central machine, but customers experience every part together.

Fourth, founders with complementary abilities can be powerful. Ibuka's technical curiosity helped produce unusual ideas. Morita's commercial confidence helped turn an internal experiment into a global product despite opposition. Neither engineering nor marketing alone explains the Walkman's success.

Finally, the story shows the value of reusing proven technology intelligently. Sony did not have enough time to invent a completely new mechanism. By adapting the Pressman and combining it with new headphones, the team lowered risk and moved quickly. Innovation is sometimes the art of connecting existing parts around a fresh understanding of the user.

Conclusion

Sony grew from a small post-war workshop into a global electronics company by repeatedly asking how technology could change daily life. The Walkman expressed this approach perfectly. It did not invent recorded music, cassette tape, batteries, or headphones. It brought these elements together in a product that made music personal and mobile.

Masaru Ibuka supplied the original practical need, Sony's engineers transformed an existing recorder, and Akio Morita insisted that a real market was waiting. Their decision changed how people used public space and helped prepare society for later portable devices. The Walkman reminds us that a notable product is not always the one with the most features. Sometimes it is the one that gives people a new freedom.
